## What it is
The program that builds the training data of the finding assistant ([[radiology-report-matcher]]) for three imaging methods: ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). A "finding" is one named observation on an image, such as a pleural effusion. A "training corpus" is a file of short texts, each labelled with the finding it describes; the learned matcher of [[radiology-finding-engine]] reads such a file to learn which words go with which finding. The X-ray corpus is built by an older program, `analysis/training_corpus.py`; this one does the same job for the other methods.

The page also covers two later products of the same area of work, which the project calls the "corpus lane" (a lane is an area of work that one session owns): the descriptor schemas of the three methods, and the tables of related diagnoses for all four methods including X-ray. Both were added on 2026-11-14 and are described under "What it does". The work of 2026-11-15 is under "Changes of 2026-11-15". The page describes commit `c1bc2c9` of 2026-11-15; work that is not committed is not described.

## What it does
For each method it produces three things: a list of findings (the inventory, in the format of [[radiology-finding-inventory]]), a corpus file of labelled texts, and a manifest. The manifest is a small committed file with counts, sources, licences and checksums (a checksum is a fingerprint that changes when the file changes) but no text; the corpus text itself stays in the `data/` folder, which is not in git.

Two rules shape it, both decided by Giovanni on 2026-11-14 and recorded in the decision log of `DESIGN.md` and in requirement AN-70 (a requirement is a numbered promise in `docs/REQUIREMENTS.md` that a test must check):

- The corpus must cross several public sources and never be a copy of one. The texts come from three: the "tuning half" of the Radiopaedia teaching cases (the cases are split in two halves; only the tuning half may train anything, the other half is kept for the one-time test), imaging sentences of case reports in PubMed Central (PMC, a free archive of journal articles) whose own file states the licence CC BY or CC0 (licences that allow copying and adapting), and sentences of the open-access IDKD books (a Springer series of radiology course books under CC BY 4.0). The build refuses a corpus in which one source holds more than 60 % of the rows.
- It must use few model tokens (a token is the unit in which a language model's use is counted). So the finding lists are made from data that was already paid for, and model calls are batched.

Results on 2026-11-14 (tuning half only, not a test score):

- Ultrasound: 2,732 rows (1,395 quotes and 1,337 rewrites) for 338 of the 750 findings of its list; Radiopaedia 54 %, PubMed Central 38 %, IDKD books 8 %.
- CT: 2,668 rows (1,376 quotes and 1,292 rewrites) for 493 of 1,180 findings; Radiopaedia 60 % after 135 rows were dropped to respect the limit, PubMed Central 32 %, IDKD books 8 %.
- MRI: 3,419 rows (1,790 quotes and 1,629 rewrites) for 502 of 1,107 findings; Radiopaedia 60 % after 834 rows were dropped to respect the limit, PubMed Central 34 %, IDKD books 6 %.
- Hit rate "right finding among three proposals", measured by five-fold cross-validation (the corpus is cut in five parts by diagnosis; four train, one is scored, in turn), with a simulated user for the rewrites. Engine alone, then engine plus learned matcher: ultrasound 71.5 % and 77.3 % on quotes, 19.8 % and 67.9 % on rewrites; CT 77.9 % and 72.7 % on quotes, 25.6 % and 56.2 % on rewrites; MRI 70.8 % and 74.9 % on quotes, 24.6 % and 60.7 % on rewrites. All are below the project targets (90 % on proper descriptions and 90 % on rewrites after questions, `docs/SCOPE.md`), and in CT the matcher lowers the quotes: its weight is the X-ray value and is not tuned per method. The numbers are entry H10 of `docs/results-registry.json`.

### Descriptor schemas (2026-11-14)
A "descriptor" is an attribute of a finding that a proper description states, such as its shape or its margin, with a fixed set of standard values ("round", "oval", ...). The set of descriptors of a finding is its "schema". The question dialog of [[radiology-finding-engine]] asks about descriptors, so a list without schemas gives it nothing to ask. The X-ray list got its schemas from a literature review per finding; for about 3,000 findings of the three other methods that was out of reach in model tokens, so a cheaper method was chosen and recorded in the decision log of `DESIGN.md`. Requirements AN-81 to AN-84.

- **The descriptor library** (`assistant/lexicon/descriptor-library-en.json`, commit `7594ff2`) is the vocabulary: 36 attributes with 185 standard values in all (24 attributes apply to ultrasound, 25 to CT, 27 to MRI). Every value is a class, or a combination of classes, of RadLex 4.3, named by its RadLex identifier. RadLex is the radiology vocabulary of the Radiological Society of North America, used under its licence; the library is an adaptation and is not endorsed by that society. `python3 -I analysis/modality_descriptors.py library` checks the library against the RadLex file and refuses a value RadLex does not hold, a value the dialog could never read in a text, and an attribute named like one of the dialog's own questions.
- **Which values a finding takes** (`assign`, then `apply`): two models, Haiku and Sonnet, each read every finding (name, kind, definition) with the library of its method, forty findings per call, and list the attributes that help to recognise it with every value it can show. A descriptor enters a finding only when both models named it with at least one value in common (the values are then the union of both answers), or when the finding's own name or wordings state the value and one model agrees. Values outside the library are dropped. Each descriptor cites RadLex and says in a field `chosen_by` how its values were chosen.
- **Result** (commit `d064098`, counts from `docs/descriptor-manifest-<method>.json`): ultrasound 593 of 750 findings have at least one descriptor (920 descriptors: 872 by two models, 48 by wording and one model); CT 922 of 1,180 (1,418: 1,306 and 112); MRI 825 of 1,107 (1,345: 1,257 and 88). Tokens used, Haiku then Sonnet: ultrasound 405,244 and 214,820; CT 693,283 and 345,180; MRI 633,122 and 325,230.
- **Hierarchy links** (`links`, AN-83, no model): a finding's parent is the listed finding whose name has the same head noun and fewer qualifiers ("renal cyst" under "cyst"), in the same body area or in the general list. Findings of one area under one parent are listed as easily confused with each other, up to four. Counted in the committed lists: 126, 290 and 245 findings have a parent; 79, 211 and 160 have a list of confusable findings.
- **Synonyms** (`python3 -I analysis/synonyms.py build METHOD`, AN-84): beside the wordings of the article tags, the synonym lists of the three methods now add terms of RadLex and of the Consumer Health Vocabulary (a public vocabulary of everyday medical wording) when a whole name matches: 284 added terms for ultrasound, 369 for CT, 301 for MRI.

The limit of this method, as the manifests state it: the vocabulary is sourced, but which values a finding takes is the agreement of two models and was not checked against a source per finding. It can be redone finding by finding.

Effect so far, tuning half only, simulated, not a test score (from the commit message of `d064098`; the results registry was not updated with it): for CT with five folds, the word-matching engine alone finds the right finding among three for 78.3 % of quotes and 26.4 % of hurried rewrites (77.8 % and 26.2 % before the schemas), and the engine with the learned matcher for 89.0 % and 55.6 % (88.7 % and 55.3 % before). A probe of 150 CT rewrites through the question dialog gave 78.7 % after the questions with the schemas and 78.0 % without. The plan note says it in one sentence: the dialog does not gain from the schemas yet. The CT figures with the matcher are higher than the 72.7 % of the first measurement above because the matcher was changed in between ([[radiology-finding-engine]]); the registry entry H10 still holds the first measurement.

### Related diagnoses (2026-11-14)
Giovanni decided on 2026-11-14 that the corpus is the project's only database: anything the product shows is built from it and never from a second store (decision log of `DESIGN.md`). The first such product is a table of "related diagnoses" per finding, which the website shows under each proposed finding ([[radiology-website]]). Requirement AN-85.

`python3 -I analysis/training_corpus.py related METHOD` writes `assistant/lexicon/<method>-related-diagnoses-en.json` for X-ray, ultrasound, CT or MRI from the tuning-half corpus of that method. Per finding the table holds at most ten diagnoses, each with its plain name and four numbers (corpus rows, distinct sources, distinct texts, and "lift"). It holds no text and no case number.

The first version (commit `778aabd`) listed every diagnosis seen with a finding. The website session reported two faults: a diagnosis that only repeated the finding's name was listed, and one case that shows several things linked its diagnosis to all of them. The rule was made stricter (commit `87d9fc5`). A diagnosis is now related to a finding only when:

- at least two different texts show the finding under that diagnosis (a quote and its hurried rewrite count as one text), and
- the finding goes with the diagnosis more often than overall. That is the "lift": the share of the finding's rows that carry the diagnosis, divided by the share of all rows that carry it; it must be above 1.

The finding's own name and synonyms are left out, and the entries are ordered by lift, then rows, then name.

Findings with at least one related diagnosis, counted in the committed tables: X-ray 74 of 233 (186 under the first rule), ultrasound 34 of 750 (254), CT 20 of 1,180 (385), MRI 40 of 1,107 (349). These tables are built from the tuning half and are not a score of anything.

Two limits. Every link rests on a single source: only the corpus rows that come from Radiopaedia cases carry a diagnosis name (rows from case reports and books carry only an identifier of their text), so no link is confirmed by two sources yet. And the numbers say how often the corpus saw a pair, not how likely a diagnosis is, which is why the website shows names only.

### Changes of 2026-11-15

- **Closed groups** (commit `c0cd88b`, requirement AN-88). Every finding belongs to a "group", the kind of finding the dialog can ask the user about ("is it a mass, a fluid collection, ...?"). The list builder had given free group words, which is too many choices to ask about. Each body region now has a closed set of at most 16 groups, named in plain English. The table is `assistant/lexicon/finding-groups-en.json`; each group is anchored to RadLex classes, and each finding records how it was placed (`group_word`, `group_basis`). `python3 -I analysis/modality_corpus.py groups METHOD` applies the table (code in `analysis/modality_inventory.py`). `tests/test_finding_groups.py` holds five tests of it: every finding of the three lists has exactly one group of the closed set of its body region, with a plain name; a faulty table is refused (too many groups, a word in two groups, a group without a RadLex class); and, where the RadLex file is on the machine, the classes named exist in RadLex.
- **Variants are grouped, never merged** (same commit, requirement AN-106). A "variant" is a finding whose name is another finding's name plus a qualifier that RadLex states as a descriptor: in the CT list, "exophytic mass" and "infiltrative mass" are variants of "mass". Giovanni's rule of 2026-11-15, as the decision log of `DESIGN.md` records it: variant entries of a finding are not merged; they are grouped as variants of the characterisation of the same finding, inside the same imaging method. Each method has its own nomenclature and is evaluated separately; nothing is merged, shared or mapped across methods. A first plan to collapse variants into their base finding was rejected by him. So each variant keeps its identifier, name and labels and gains a field `variant_of` with the base finding of the same method, the qualifier and the RadLex class. `docs/variant-families.json` counts the families (a base finding with its variants): ultrasound 20 families with 48 variants among 750 findings, the largest with 23; CT 37 families with 71 variants among 1,180 findings, the largest with 9; MRI 35 families with 57 variants among 1,107 findings, the largest with 4. ## How it does it
The steps run in order; each can be run again and continues where it stopped (for example after the account's usage limit). `tools/modality_corpus_run.sh` runs steps 4 to 9 for each method.

1. `candidates` (no model): the finding tags of the articles are grouped. A finding tag is a set of equivalent wordings of one finding that an earlier step extracted from an article and fact-checked against the literature (step T2 of [[radiology-analysis]]). Wordings that share a tag form a cluster. A cluster is kept when 2 articles state it for the method, or 3 when its tags name no method.
2. `inventory` (model): a model turns the clusters, 100 per call, into findings with a name, a body area, a group and a one-line definition, and a second call merges findings that are the same thing under two names. Each finding cites the literature link stored with its tags, or the tagged article when there is none. The definition is written by the model and marked as such. These lists had no descriptor schemas at this step; they were added afterwards by a separate program (see "Descriptor schemas" above), and that program's `apply` and `links` steps must be run again whenever this step rebuilds a list.
3. `pmc` (no model): searches Europe PMC for English open-access case reports, reads each article file, keeps it only when the file itself states CC BY or CC0, and keeps only sentences of case or imaging sections and figure captions that mention the method and a finding word and carry no personal context (age, sex, admission). Articles of the internet reports kept for the final test are excluded.
4. `sample` (no model): picks the texts, 45 % Radiopaedia cases, 35 % PMC passages, 20 % book sentences, and guesses the body area of each text with the word-matching engine.
5. `annotate` (model): two annotators (the models Sonnet and Haiku) each read every text, ten texts per call, with only the finding lists of the text's body areas plus the general list. Each names the findings the text states and quotes the words. A quote that is not in the text is discarded.
6. A row exists only when both annotators state the same finding for the same text on the same list; its text is their quotes.
7. `rewrite` (model): each agreed quote is rewritten the way a hurried non-expert would type it, and a second model checks that the rewrite still shows the finding; failed ones are dropped.
8. `build`: writes the corpus and the manifest. It refuses rows that are not of the tuning half and articles of the held-out internet reports, and it drops whole texts of the largest source, in a fixed order, until that source holds at most 60 % of the rows (a corpus with a single source is refused).
9. `crosscheck` (no model) counts, for each finding, in how many independent source families its name or a synonym occurs: article tags, literature link, RadLex (the radiology vocabulary of the Radiological Society of North America), IDKD books, PMC reports. For ultrasound 707 of 750 findings occur in two or more, for CT 1,115 of 1,180, for MRI 1,039 of 1,107. `score` runs the cross-validation described above.

Haiku is the second annotator, not Codex (OpenAI's coding agent, used elsewhere in the project): a Codex call measured about 15,000 tokens before any text, a Haiku call about 3,000. The cost is that the two annotators are less independent than two models of different makers. Measured token use per method, from the manifests: ultrasound about 5.4 million, CT about 5.5 million, MRI about 6.9 million.

## Functions
- `modality_inventory.clusters(mod)`: clusters of equivalent wordings for a method from the finding tags.
- `modality_inventory.write_inventory(mod, entries, by_id)`: the area files `assistant/modules/<method>/<area>.json` and the synonym file `assistant/lexicon/<method>-synonyms-en.json`.
- `pmc_passage(xml, mod)`: the usable imaging sentences of one PMC article, or the reason it is refused.
- `held_out_pmcids()`: the PMC article numbers of the reports kept for the final test.
- `route(engine, text)`: the one or two body areas of a text.
- `agreed_pairs(mod, annotators)`: the (text, finding) pairs both annotators stated.
- `balance(rows)`: drops texts of the largest source until it holds at most 60 % of the rows.
- `load(mod)`: the corpus rows, after the checksum and tuning-half checks.
- `modality_descriptors.library_problems`, `merge`, `links` (in `analysis/modality_descriptors.py`): the problems of the library against RadLex; the descriptors of one finding from two models' answers and its own wordings; the parent and confusable findings from the names.
- `modality_topup.thin_findings`, `finding_phrases`, `phrase_pattern`, `cmd_topup` (in `analysis/modality_topup.py`): the findings with thin training data; the wordings of each finding; the search pattern for them; the top-up command.
- `training_corpus.related_diagnoses`, `build_related` (in `analysis/training_corpus.py`): the related diagnoses of each finding under the two-texts and lift rule, and the table file of one method.

## Where it sits in the pipeline
After the article finding tags and beside the X-ray inventory ([[radiology-finding-inventory]]); before the engine work on these methods. Since 2026-11-15 it also receives the lists of missing findings from the development batch of real reports ([[radiology-real-report-factcheck]]), and has added them to its lists. In the project plan (`PLAN.md`) it is the "corpus" lane; its milestone "Ultrasound, CT and MRI training data" closed on 2026-11-14. The descriptor schemas are the data side of the later milestone "Ultrasound, CT and MRI at target", which belongs to the engine work and is still open.

## Who receives its output
The learned matcher and the question dialog of [[radiology-finding-engine]], which read the corpora and the schemas; the website ([[radiology-website]]), which reads the tables of related diagnoses; and Giovanni, who reads the manifests.
