## What it is
The map is the home screen of the wiki: every page is a dot coloured by its kind, with lines for the "feeds" relation (arrows: what feeds what) and the "mentions" relation (dashed: one page links to another), and four information boxes below it.

## What it does
Hovering over a dot lights it and its neighbours in gold and dims the rest; the boxes show What it is, What it does, Fed by and Who receives its output (and keep the last dot when the mouse leaves). Clicking a dot opens its article. You drag to turn the globe and use the wheel or a two-finger pinch to zoom (on a touch screen one finger turns it, so that the point of the globe under the finger stays under it, and two fingers pinch around their midpoint; nothing else zooms, so a double tap, quick taps or circles drawn with a finger never zoom the map); the project filter and the timeline hide what does not belong. The highlight is quick (every fade lasts 0.07 seconds). On a large window the map takes all the height that the four boxes do not need, so the page never scrolls when a dot is hovered; a text longer than its box scrolls inside it.

The map is alive. Each dot rests where the layout puts it but can be pushed away from that place and then springs back, with a little wobble, like a weight on elastic: pressing a dot and moving the mouse drags it, its neighbours stretch along behind it and contract back when you let go (a click without movement still opens the page, and a drag never does). On a phone the dots are slightly less elastic (a stiffer spring, less following of neighbours, more friction) and tapping a dot draws its neighbours in a little, as hovering does on a desktop. Hovering a dot draws its neighbours a fifth of the way towards it (at most about 14 pixels). Each dot answers the mouse where it rests, not where it is drawn at that moment, so the movement can never slide a dot under a still mouse and make the highlight flicker. While the globe is being turned, a dot is being dragged or fingers are down, the dots that pass under the pointer are not lit at all, so a drag stays quick and nothing flickers along its way. A dot's name answers the mouse only while it is at rest and no other dot is lit, and the detail that depends on position (labels, dashed lines) is decided from the resting place, so a neighbour that is drawn towards the lit dot can never take the highlight from it. When the page first loads the dots spring open from the middle, and after the window is resized they glide from where they were to their new places instead of jumping.

The map is a globe seen in perspective, on a desktop and on a phone alike: every page is a point on a ball and one camera looks at it, with north always up. The middle faces you (dots are bigger and further apart, and get a label as you zoom in: at the first view only the projects are labelled, and a name is written only if it does not overlap one already written, so the names never pile up: `MapLabels` takes the dots that deserve a name best first (projects, then the ones nearest the middle, ties by id so that nothing flickers), estimates each name's box (0.55 of the font size per character, below its dot) and skips a name that would touch an earlier one; skipped names appear as you zoom in and make room, LB-1 and LB-2) and the surface turns away towards the rim (dots are smaller, closer, flattened into ovals along the direction of the turn, fading out near the horizon, and without labels, and the dashed "mentions" lines are left out there unless one of their dots is lit). A dot that is turned past the rim is on the far side of the ball and is not drawn. A faint shading and a few curved lines of latitude and longitude show the sphere; they are part of the globe and turn with it. Dragging the globe turns it so that the point under the pointer stays under it; the wheel and the pinch zoom about the pointer, from the whole globe (1) up to 12 times. The dots are small (at the first view an ordinary dot is at most 9 pixels wide, a project 14) and they spread as you zoom: the middle dot grows at most 2.2 times from the first view to the deepest zoom (its growth follows the zoom to the power 0.4 and stops at 2 times), while the distances between dots grow with the zoom itself, so a dot's width against the distance to its neighbour falls at every step (DY-13; on the real phone the old dots were 30 pixels wide after one pinch, 8 to 10 now, with the same spacing). The nearer-than-the-rim size cue (dots at the middle are up to 1.6 times those at the border) compares a dot with the middle at the current zoom, not with the first view, which is what used to make every dot grow at once when zooming. A dot answers the pointer within its drawn size plus 8 pixels. The ball starts fitting the free area exactly. The first part of the zoom makes the ball grow with it, up to about 1.7 times (the edges of the area then cut off part of the ball, and on a phone it reaches past the sides of the screen); from there the camera moves in and zooming spreads the dots further apart, so the dots in the cut-off part come into view when you turn the globe. On a phone, while a dot is selected the ball shrinks into the part of the screen that the balloon leaves free, and the selected dot is turned to its middle. Dragging on the map never selects text: a dot's name can be selected only by pressing the mouse on the name itself and dragging over it (that press does not turn the globe, move the dot or open the page).

On a phone (a window 760 pixels wide or less) the map fills the whole screen, as in Google Maps: a search bar shaped like a pill floats on top with the menu button (map, glossary, log out) inside it, and a row of filter chips sits under it. Hovering does not exist on a touch screen, so tapping a dot opens a balloon at the bottom with the same four texts, buttons for the pages that feed it or receive its output (tapping one jumps to that dot), and a button that opens its article; the close button or another dot replaces it. A phone repaints slowly, so there the map has no fades and no dashed lines (measured on the phone before the first globe was added: 118 milliseconds a frame after a tap with them and 19 without); the page also tells the browser not to offer its own translation, and choosing a search result closes the keyboard so that it does not hide the balloon.

## How it does it
Positions are computed once with a force simulation (run a few steps per animation frame, so that a slow phone keeps answering instead of freezing for about 3 seconds while the map is laid out) ([force-directed layout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force-directed_graph_drawing), done with the library elm-visualization): connected pages pull together, all pages push apart and keep a minimum gap, projects start in clusters, and a pull towards a horizontal line makes the cloud settle wide and flat. `Layout.matchAspect` then stretches it towards the shape of the strip that is wrapped round the globe (stretching only, at most 3 times, so no two dots get closer), and `MapCamera.fit` puts every dot on the sphere as a unit vector (a direction of length 1) once per layout, with an equal-area map projection (`Globe.embed`). `MapSize` decides the heights: on a wide, tall enough window the boxes get the height their text needs (about 226 pixels at 1920 pixels wide) and the map gets the rest; on a phone the map takes the whole screen height and the four boxes are replaced by the balloon (`MapView.sheet`); on a shorter or narrower window the map keeps its 58 % share and the boxes size themselves. Filters are pure functions (`Filter.visible`). The elastic layer (`Motion`) gives every dot a displacement east and north on the sphere (in thousandths of a radian) with a spring back to its place, a spring towards the average displacement of its neighbours and friction, and asks the browser for animation frames only while something is still moving. The camera (`Globe`; `GlobeGrab` holds what the pointer does to it) is three numbers: longitude, latitude and zoom. Once per frame it works out one camera (four sine and cosine calls and a 3 by 3 matrix); each dot then costs a matrix product, a comparison with the horizon (a dot on the far side stops there) and, for the dots that are seen, a closed form for the shape of the dot, with no trigonometry per dot. A dot is drawn as a circle under an SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics, a format for drawings made of shapes) `matrix` that flattens it into an oval. The SVG declares a `viewBox` equal to its size and starts at the top-left of the window (the search bar floats over it), so the pointer positions the browser reports are positions in the picture. `Touch` turns the globe under one finger and zooms about the midpoint of two.

Drawing is split so that a frame costs only what has moved. `MapGeometry` works out, once for a given set of inputs (the layout, the camera, the project filter and the date), everything about the dots at rest: where the camera puts each dot, how big it is drawn, whether it has a label, its colour and title, and which dashed lines are left out. The model keeps the result as `geometry`, and `MapCamera.refreshGeometry` runs after every message and rebuilds it only when one of those inputs has changed, which hovering, dragging a dot and the springs do not do. The displacement of the dots is sparse in what it shows: a dot that no spring reaches has a displacement of exactly zero and is drawn where it rests. So in each frame `MapView.map` places again only the displaced dots (exactly, nothing is approximated) and draws the others from the stored result through `Svg.Lazy` (an Elm function that skips redrawing a piece of the picture when its inputs are the very same values as in the previous frame). There are no invisible hit areas in the page: `MapCamera.hitAt` (through `MapGeometry.hitAt`) finds the dot under the pointer from the stored resting places, the nearest dot within its drawn radius plus 8 pixels, so turning the globe rewrites nothing for the pointer, and the click opens a page only if the mouse was pressed on the same dot (`MapCamera.clickOpens`). `MapCamera.pointerMoved` is the rule that a moving pointer lights the dot under it and puts the light out when it leaves, except while the globe is turned, a dot is dragged or fingers are down. While a dot is lit, `MapView.map` gives the map the class `focus` and gives only the lit dot and its neighbours the class `near` (only the lit dot's name is forced; the neighbours keep theirs only if it was chosen); the stylesheet dims everything else and stops the other dots' labels from answering the pointer, so lighting a dot changes the classes of about forty elements, not of two hundred dots and a thousand lines. The lines between dots are not one page element each: `MapEdges` draws the lines that are not lit as a few `path` elements, one per kind (feeds or mentions) and per stroke width in steps of a quarter pixel (at most 14 in all), and the arrowheads of the feeding ones as small filled triangles in one more path per group, because an SVG marker (the way the arrowhead used to be drawn) belongs to the last point of a path and not to each segment. The lines of the lit dot stay single elements with the marker, since they are few. On the real phone the single lines cost about a fifth of a frame, mostly the browser's painting on its main thread; the paths took a swipe frame from 13.1 to 9.6 ms (see [[map-benchmark]]). `svg.map` has `will-change: transform`, which gives the map a layer of its own so that the browser does not draw the rest of the page again when the map changes. `Motion.step` keeps the state of the springs in one array with one entry per dot and steps all of them in a single pass: the average of a dot's neighbours is the neighbour matrix (each row divided by its sum) times the displacement vector, read straight from the array; a dot that nothing reaches comes out exactly at zero, with every dot at rest and nothing pulling or holding nothing is done, and when everything has settled the small leftovers are dropped. A hover starts the animation frames at once; an unhover starts them only if some dot is displaced. The neighbours of the lit dot come from the neighbour list that the springs already use (`Motion.adjacency`), not from a scan of all lines. The project filter, the timeline slider, the legend, the four boxes and the phone's balloon are skipped in the same way while their inputs have not changed. Positions and sizes written into the picture are rounded to a tenth of a pixel, so the text that the browser reads and compares is short. The maths is tested against reference numbers (`GlobeTests`, 78 checks to a billionth (1e-9) against vectors made by the reference program of the spec, kept in `GlobeVectors`), the array version of the springs against the dictionary version it replaced (`MotionTests`; the old code stays in the tests as `MotionOld`), and the behaviour of the map, the touch rules and the pointer rules in `WikiTests`. How this was measured is written in `docs/MEASURING-THE-MAP.md` of the wiki repository, and the tools that take the measurements are described on [[map-benchmark]]. Why the map became a camera on a sphere, with the numbers, the tests that were rewritten and the one open question (the drawing time of zooming on other threads rose; it comes from the removed discs), is in `docs/adr/0010-globe-camera-replaces-lens.md`.
