## What it is
The map is the home screen of the wiki: every page is a dot coloured by its kind, with lines for the "feeds" relation (arrows: what feeds what) and the "mentions" relation (dashed: one page links to another), and four information boxes below it.

## What it does
Hovering over a dot lights it and its neighbours in gold and dims the rest; the boxes show What it is, What it does, Fed by and Who receives its output (and keep the last dot when the mouse leaves). Clicking a dot opens its article. You drag to pan, and use the wheel or a two-finger pinch to zoom (on a touch screen one finger pans, so that the map point under the finger stays under it, and two fingers pinch around their midpoint; nothing else zooms, so a double tap, quick taps or circles drawn with a finger never zoom the map); the project filter and the timeline hide what does not belong. The highlight is quick (every fade lasts 0.07 seconds). On a large window the map takes all the height that the four boxes do not need, so the page never scrolls when a dot is hovered; a text longer than its box scrolls inside it.

The map is alive. Each dot rests where the layout puts it but can be pushed away from that place and then springs back, with a little wobble, like a weight on elastic: pressing a dot and moving the mouse drags it, its neighbours stretch along behind it and contract back when you let go (a click without movement still opens the page, and a drag never does). On a phone the dots are slightly less elastic (a stiffer spring, less following of neighbours, more friction) and tapping a dot draws its neighbours in a little, as hovering does on a desktop. Hovering a dot draws its neighbours a fifth of the way towards it (at most about 14 pixels). Each dot answers the mouse where it rests, not where it is drawn at that moment, so the movement can never slide a dot under a still mouse and make the highlight flicker. When the page first loads the dots spring open from the middle, and after the window is resized they glide from where they were to their new places instead of jumping.

The map is seen through a lens shaped like a globe, on a desktop and on a phone alike: the cloud of dots is wrapped onto a ball seen from the front. The layout is first reshaped to be about as tall as it is wide and its square is bent into a disc, so the dots fill a round ball and the rim is as populated as the middle. The middle faces you (dots are bigger, further apart and labelled) and the surface turns away towards the rim (dots are smaller, closer, flattened into ovals along the direction of the turn, and without labels, and the dashed "mentions" lines are left out there); the rim is about three times tighter than the middle, which is what makes it read as a ball. A dot that pans or zooms past the rim is on the far side of the ball and is not drawn. A faint shading and a few curved lines of latitude and longitude show the sphere; they belong to the glass and stay still while you pan. On a phone the ball is as wide as the screen; while a dot is selected the ball shrinks into the part of the screen that the balloon leaves free, and the selected dot sits in its middle.

On a phone (a window 760 pixels wide or less) the map fills the whole screen, as in Google Maps: a search bar shaped like a pill floats on top with the menu button (map, glossary, language, log out) inside it, and a row of filter chips sits under it. Hovering does not exist on a touch screen, so tapping a dot opens a balloon at the bottom with the same four texts, buttons for the pages that feed it or receive its output (tapping one jumps to that dot), and a button that opens its article; the close button or another dot replaces it. A phone repaints slowly, so there the map has no fades and no dashed lines (measured on the phone before the lens was added: 118 milliseconds a frame after a tap with them and 19 without); the page also tells the browser not to offer its own translation, and choosing a search result closes the keyboard so that it does not hide the balloon.

## How it does it
Positions are computed once with a force simulation (run a few steps per animation frame, so that a slow phone keeps answering instead of freezing for about 3 seconds while the map is laid out) ([force-directed layout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force-directed_graph_drawing), done with the library elm-visualization): connected pages pull together, all pages push apart and keep a minimum gap, projects start in clusters, and a pull towards a horizontal line makes the cloud settle wide and flat. `Layout.matchAspect` then reshapes it to the shape of the map area (stretching only, at most 3 times, so no two dots get closer), so the dots use the full width. `MapSize` decides the heights: on a wide, tall enough window the boxes get the height their text needs (about 226 pixels at 1920 pixels wide) and the map gets the rest; on a phone the map takes the whole screen height and the four boxes are replaced by the balloon (`MapView.sheet`); on a shorter or narrower window the map keeps its 58 % share and the boxes size themselves. Filters are pure functions (`Filter.visible`). The elastic layer (`Motion`) gives every dot a displacement with a spring back to its place, a spring towards the average displacement of its neighbours and friction, and asks the browser for animation frames only while something is still moving; the lens (`Lens`) puts a point at distance r from the centre of the ball (1 at the rim) on a sphere at the angle r·1.2 radians and draws it at sin(r·1.2)/sin(1.2), continuing in a straight line beyond about 80 degrees so that a point far outside never folds back over the sphere; it also gives each dot its flattening (from the lens's Jacobian, the matrix that says how a small step on the map becomes a step on the screen) and has an inverse so that a mouse position finds the dot under it. `MapCamera.globeFit` makes the layout round and zooms it so that the farthest dot sits just inside the rim. Zoom and pan come from the `Zoom` module of elm-visualization; the SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics, a format for drawings made of shapes) declares a `viewBox` equal to its size and starts at the top-left of the window (the search bar floats over it), so the mouse wheel, dragging and pinching all zoom around the pointer.
